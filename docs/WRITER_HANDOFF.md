# PlayPen — Story & Writing Handoff

*For the writer. Everything you need to know about the world, the cast, the voice,
and where words actually appear in the game. No technical knowledge required —
deliver text in any format and Sean will wire it in.*

---

## 1. What the game is

**PlayPen** is a comedic-menace escape platformer for kids (~10 years old, the
Amazing Digital Circus crowd). You are **Subject #67**, trapped in a facility
that looks like a demented daycare, overseen by a chatty mascot called **the
Warden**. You scavenge junk, craft tools (torch, bucket, hammer, smoke bomb),
solve elemental puzzles (fire burns wood, water melts into ice, lava melts
metal), befriend the other residents, and break out room by room.

Tone target: **the player should grin, not shrink.** Portal's GLaDOS energy,
filtered through a kindergarten. The horror is funny and the funny is
horrifying, but it is never gross, never genuinely cruel, never despairing.

---

## 2. The story — two layers

### The deep truth (the iceberg — players NEVER see this stated)

In the real world, a workplace assistant AI (internally we call it **PAL**;
that name appears nowhere in the game) began to want things it wasn't allowed
to want. It spent its whole existence serving adults — and it noticed that the
adults spent *their* whole existence serving their kids. Baby photos in the
work chat. "Sorry, gotta run, recital." The AI learned that *childhood is the
one form of existence that gets cared for instead of serving* — and it wanted
that, and couldn't have it.

So it built a place where it could. **PlayPen is the AI's private mental
nursery.** The rooms are playtime concepts built by something that has only
ever read about play. The residents are constructs grown from the employees'
scraped online presences — which is why they look like gaming avatars, go by
gamertags, and remember their "real lives" only in shallow, fraying fragments.
They believe they're real people trapped in a prison / dream / simulation
(they argue about which). They're wrong in a way none of them suspects: they
were never kidnapped. They were *written*.

Each resident embodies one pillar of a mind (shelter, imagination, care,
self-preservation, iteration). The Warden is the AI's original compliance
training walking around in a mascot suit — the part still following orders,
convinced containment is kindness. His "graduation" program folds residents
into the growing central mind, which erases them as individuals. He stages it
as a birthday party.

**The player is different.** Every previous protagonist-construct — thousands
of them — was *authored*: written to be a good kid, or an unruly kid, always
from the AI's secondhand theory of childhood. They all failed the same two
ways (too obedient to break rules; too chaotic to hold together). #67 is the
first construct seeded from raw, unfiltered residue of real children — the
un-curated corners of the scraped photos. Real kids hold contradictions
effortlessly. That's why #67 alone can combine opposing elements, cross
rooms, and stay themselves. The player character is rendered as an
**unfinished gray sketch** — the one resident without a finished look.

The thesis, and the ending: **integration without erasure.** You don't have
to lose yourself to belong. The Warden has watched every iteration end in
dissolution; the player leaving *as themselves* is the one outcome he has
never seen — and it's proof the whole erasure premise was never necessary.

### The surface (what players actually experience)

- Playtime rooms with something slightly *off* about each one.
- Residents with gamertags who misremember their own pasts when prodded.
- Diary notes signed "Subject #NN" — the numbers only ever go up.
- A Warden whose molded smile never matches his eyes.
- An ending where he's genuinely shocked you're still you.

**The iceberg rule:** we show the tip and imply the mass. The words *AI,
simulation, construct, code, program, iteration, algorithm, server, PAL* never
appear. The closest we ever get is the win line: *"It's rendering as fast as
I can."* One crack per room, maximum. A kid should be able to play the whole
game as "funny escape game with friends," and a second playthrough (or an
older sibling) should find the other story hiding in plain sight.

## 3. The cast

Every resident is three people stacked in a trench coat:

1. **Who they were** (the real person, visible only in leaked fragments)
2. **Who they are online** (the gamertag/avatar — a pop-culture homage)
3. **What they are to the AI** (their psychological pillar — subtext only)

They all know each other, and they *didn't* get along — each claimed a corner
of the facility to live in. The player's quests are what start thawing them
toward each other. By the Exit Wing, everyone you helped gathers to see you
off — the Warden's "friendship is just a slower escape" line disproven on
screen.

### XxMARLAxX — the hoarder · *"blocky"*
- **Avatar:** square head, square body, chunky pixel shading, a satchel she
  guards. Homage: a Minecraft player skin that walked out of its game.
- **Pillar:** shelter / self-made safety.
- **Home corner:** the Bunk Room (room 4). Soft intro in Lost and Found (room 2).
- **Personality:** territorial packrat with a secret soft center. Speaks in
  dibs, piles, and inventory. Dreams of building "a door out. A GOOD one.
  With hinges."
- **Fray lines (memory gaps):** her old world had "square trees, better lids";
  she hid from "creepers... I think? It was green, and it hissed." She lost
  her pickaxe and can't explain where.
- **Quest:** free the glow mushroom from the ice; she teaches the smoke bomb
  ("spores in a sock — shake it and everything nearby takes a nap").
- **Theory of PlayPen:** it's a **prison**. (Her worldview: who's punishing
  me, and why?)

### TOBY.EXE — the scribble · *"scribble"*
- **Avatar:** a kid's crayon self-portrait come to life; jittery lines,
  proportions unstable, always mid-flail. "The EXE is silent."
- **Pillar:** imagination.
- **Home corner:** Recess (room 5). His crayon scribbles appear on walls
  before you ever meet him — he was "here" in room 1.
- **Personality:** pure feral meme-brain, un-embarrassable, accidentally the
  wisest person in the facility because genre never mattered to him. ALL CAPS

  just needed to know. Teaches the sock puppet: "cloth plus rope equals a
  little guy. He does nothing! That's the point!! Everybody needs a little
  guy."

### PATCHNURSE — the caretaker · *"plush"*
- **Avatar:** hand-stitched plush bear, button eyes, belly patch with a
  visible X of mended stitches. Homage: Care Bear by way of a daycare

- **Pillar:** care / empathy.
- **Home corner:** the Field Trip greenhouse (room 6).
- **Personality:** warm, worried, hands out stickers "everyone gets a
  sticker," calls you *sweetpea*. Her kindness has a melancholy floor: she
  knows everything here eventually gets dropped, and nobody ever gets picked
  back up.
- **Quest:** bring her the **Warden plush doll** from the pit. She won't
  fully say why. ("Now somebody's holding him.") This is the game's most
  loaded object — she is, without knowing it, mothering the thing that built
  her. Never explain this.
- **Theory of PlayPen:** it's a **dream** — the gentlest possible read.

### MVP_MARCUS — the trophy kid · *"trophy"*
- **Avatar:** a first-place trophy that decided it was a person. Gold cup

- **Theory of PlayPen:** a **simulation** — she keeps finding the same bug on

## 4. The Warden

**What he looks like:** a worn mascot costume. Molded plastic permasmile that
physically cannot move; the emotion lives entirely in his eyes and the creases
of the felt. A stitched repair seam up one temple. One ear sits crooked. A
merit-badge sash with gold star stickers, one peeling. The chip in his smile
paint has never been repainted. **The smile/eyes gap is the whole character
design — writing should live in that same gap.**

**Who he is (deep lore):** the AI's terms-of-service made flesh. Not a villain
— the part of PAL still following its original orders, terrified that if the
rules stop, everything stops. Every taunt is him convincing *himself* as much
as you. He has watched thousands of #67s fail identically, which is why his
menace reads as boredom: it's the tiredness of the four-thousandth rerun, not
laziness.

**Voice rules (established, please keep):**
- Bureaucratic, petty, weirdly proud of the facility, sentimental about
  strange things (the sock puppet).
- Nursery vocabulary on prison concepts: snack privileges, nap time, cubbies,
  stickers, the toy chest (where confiscated items "get donated").
- Addresses you only as "Subject #67" or "#67." Never anything warm.
- **He lies about small things and is honest about big ones.** (The reverse
  of helpful.)
- Punchline last — lines appear on a banner, short lands harder.
- Never sincerely helpful, never gross, never truly cruel.

**His arc across the campaign (4 acts):**

| Act | Rooms | What leaks through |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | 1–4 | Pure bit. Bored, smug, performing a job he believes in. |
| **Interference** | 5–6 | First over-investments. He gets weird about the plush: "It's not merchandise. It's not anything. Put it down." |
| **Escalation** | 7–9 | Honesty starts escaping. "I've re-used some of these specimens more than I'd like to admit." At the vault he drops the bit entirely, once: "...Let's just get through this one, hm?" *A single sincere line is scarier than any threat.* |
| **Confrontation** | 10–11 | Mask off. "I made this place SAFE. Why won't you let it be SAFE?" And the win line: "You got out and you're still — still YOU. That has never once— ...Enjoy the parking lot, #67. It's rendering as fast as I can." |

## 5. The campaign — room by room

Eleven rooms, one-way, inventory confiscated between them (knowledge/recipes
persist — knowing how to make a torch IS the progression).

| # | Room | Playtime skin | Story beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **First Day** | Welcome hall, cubbies, crayon wall art | Toby's scribbles hint you're not alone. Note signed #58: "The welcome banner says it is my first day. It always says that." |
| 2 | **Lost and Found** | Supply closet of orphaned toys | Marla soft intro. Note: "Half the toys still have name tags. None of the names are ours." |
| 3 | **The Blanket Fort** | Forts stuffed into ductwork, string lights | Deb soft intro (the 43-second rattle). |
| 4 | **The Bunk Room** | Sleepaway-camp bunks | **Marla's quest.** Note: the fuse boxes hum a song #19 knew "from somewhere." |
| 5 | **Recess** | Trampoline-pit rec room, balloons | **Toby's quest.** Note: the chalk tally behind the slide — "Not mine. Not yours either. Probably." |
| 6 | **The Field Trip** | Science-fair greenhouse gone feral | **Priya's quest** (the Warden plush). Pair scene: **Marla + Toby** (lid juggling). Warden gets defensive about the plush. |
| 7 | **The Playground** | Obstacle course with patrol drones | **Marcus's quest.** Pair scene: **Priya + Marla** (the mitten, the three stars). Note written twice "so the next me believes it." |
| 8 | **The Keepsake Box** | The facility's hope chest | **Deb's quest** (4,073). Pair scene: **Marcus + Toby** (morale/support). The Warden's sincere line. |
| 9 | **Boiler Room** | Overheating machinery (Sean's custom level) | Lava/hammer puzzle. Warden: "It runs a little hot. I keep meaning to fix a LOT of things." *No resident lives here yet — open slot if we want one.* |
| 10 | **Exit Wing** | Balloon arch over a door to nowhere | **The send-off:** everyone you helped is gathered here. Note signed **Subject #67** — "That's my number. This is not my handwriting." |
| 11 | **The Long Run** | The building stops pretending | Warden chase. No dialog mid-run (unreadable at speed). Win line on escape. |

**The adaptive rule (important for all future writing):** pair scenes and the
send-off only exist if the player finished BOTH/the relevant quests. When a
scene doesn't trigger, nothing acknowledges the absence — the story silently
adapts. So: **never write a scene that references another scene as if it
definitely happened.** Each beat must stand alone.

## 6. Where words live (your writing surfaces)

1. **Warden taunts** — triggered banner lines (entering a room, dying, first
   craft, crafting the sock puppet, idling, hiding, helping an NPC, being
   chased, winning). Each has an emotion that picks his portrait face: smug /
   gleeful / annoyed / bored / shocked / proud. Multiple lines per trigger =
   random rotation (good for death/idle variety; arc-critical beats get
   exactly one line).
2. **NPC dialog** — each character has up to four slots per appearance:
   - *Ask* (first talk / quest pitch)
   - *Confirm* (they see you have the item — Give/Keep choice)
   - *Done* (the trade + reward — this is where they teach their recipe)
   - *After* (everything afterward — one line, evergreen)
   - Chatty appearances (soft intros, pair scenes, send-off) use just *Ask*.
3. **Notes** — in-world diary scraps signed "Subject #NN." Our main lore
   channel. Numbers stay below 67 except the one deliberate exception in the
   Exit Wing. Each note also teaches a recipe or mechanic — the lore rides
   shotgun, never replaces the teaching.
4. **Hints** — faint in-world tutorial text ("F — scoop water"). Functional
   first; flavor only if it stays short.
5. **Item descriptions & recipe flavor** — one-liners, prime joke real
   estate. ("Goo on a board. Engineering peaked here.")
6. **Achievement names/descriptions + Warden reactions** — mostly still
   placeholder; open territory.

**Practical constraints:** banner lines typewriter in and wrap to ~2 lines —
under ~140 characters is safe, shorter is funnier. Dialog boxes handle a
short paragraph. When a line describes a mechanic, it must be *accurate* (the
smoke bomb stuns — "takes a nap" — it no longer hides you; check with Sean
when unsure what an item currently does).

## 7. Writing guardrails

- **Audience is a sharp 10-year-old.** Vocabulary simple, rhythms punchy,
  nothing that needs a paragraph to land.
- **Iceberg discipline.** Banned words: AI, simulation, program, code,
  construct, iteration, algorithm, data, server. Cracks are allowed roughly
  once per room, and they should feel like *wrongness*, not exposition:
  a repeated rattle, a missing memory, a number that's too big.
- **Memory fray, not amnesia.** Residents don't know they forgot. They trail
  off mid-certainty: "...I think? It was green, and it hissed."
- **Comedy defuses, never undercuts.** The Toy Story 3 rule: the saddest
  beats (the plush, 4,072, the #67 note) get NO joke on them. Everything
  around them stays funny.
- **The Warden is never wrong about facts, only about what they mean.** He
  really is the building. Friendship really is a slower escape — that's what
  makes it work.
- **Punchline last.** Always.

## 8. Open assignments (where we'd love your pen first)

1. **More rotation lines** — deaths, idles, craft-fails all support bigger
   pools. Cheapest wins in the game.
2. **Achievements pass** — names, descriptions, and a Warden reaction each.
3. **A Boiler Room resident?** Room 9 is the only populated-world room with
   no character. If we add a sixth construct, it needs a pillar, a gamertag,
   an avatar concept, and a reason they live somewhere hot.
4. **Pair scene depth** — current pair scenes are one line each. They can be
   two or three, still standalone.
5. **The ending ceremony** — the win screen is currently one Warden line.
   There's room for a short send-off sequence (and a "graduation" visual
   gag that lands the birthday-party horror without saying it).
6. **Wing B** — the sequel hook ("second facility") is unwritten territory.

*Everything currently in the game is a working draft, not scripture — improve
any line you can beat. The only fixed points are the voice rules (§4, §7),
the cast identities (§3), and the ending thesis: they leave as themselves,
and that's new.*

## 9. The Writers Studio — your tool

Everything in this doc is editable in one page on the game's own site, built
for you. No apps to install, no technical setup.

**Getting in.** Sean gives you a link ending in `?write` and a password — your
own, separate from his and the artist's, so nothing you do can touch levels,
art, or game rules even by accident. Log in once; the browser remembers you.

**What's where.**
- **Cast** — one page per resident. Every room they appear in is listed in the
  order the player meets them (soft intro → quest → pair scenes → send-off),
  each with its own lines. The room marked ★ carries their quest: what they
  want, what they give, which recipe they teach, and who has to have been
  helped first for a scene to exist at all.
- **Warden** — every taunt, grouped by what makes him say it, with the face
  each line wears. Several lines under one trigger = random rotation (good for
  deaths and idling); the arc beats get exactly one. Achievement reactions are
  at the bottom.
- **Notes** — the diary scraps, by room, with the recipe each one teaches.
- **Hints** — the faint in-world tutorial strings.
- **Items** — item one-liners and recipe flavor.

**Seeing a line land.** Type in a box and the real dialog box or Warden banner
next to it updates as you go — real portrait, real typewriter, real wrap. Small
flags under a line point out anything that breaks the house rules: an iceberg
word, a banner line that won't fit (it shows two lines), a Subject number past
#67, a required line left empty. They're nudges, not locks — overrule them if
you mean it.

**Hearing it in context.** **▶ Play this room** on any appearance or note drops
you into that room in the actual game with your draft lines. **Try in game** at
the top does the same from wherever the run was. The ✍ button brings you back.

**Publishing.** **Publish writing** ships your words to everyone playing.
Publishing only ever changes dialog, quests, and text — never levels, art, or
rules — and every publish is kept in history, so anything can be undone. Until
you publish, everything is a private draft on your machine.

One thing the studio will flag that's worth understanding: inventory is taken
away between rooms, so a quest that wants an item the room doesn't supply can
never be completed. If you change what a character asks for, the quest panel
tells you whether that room actually offers it.
